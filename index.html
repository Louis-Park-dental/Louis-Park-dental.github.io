Dental Assistant — Dr. Furnari's Office

I started working under Dr. Furnari in October 2025. Soph Year — Present
PT Aide
Physical Therapy Aide

I started this job late in sophomore year and have kept it running alongside everything else. Once I started my dental assistant job, I began to alternate my Saturdays. PT runs 8am to 6 or 7pm on those days.

Most of my time has been in home clinics working with elderly and sick patients, a lot of them dealing with dementia or Alzheimer's. My PT trusted me to lead certain exercises and demonstrate them directly to patients, which takes more patience than it sounds when someone doesn't always remember what you just showed them. I also led walks, assisted with in-clinic patients by anticipating what materials were needed next, prepped hot packs and equipment, and handled paperwork. It taught me how to actually be with people who need care, not just perform tasks around them.

Research

Contributor — Springer Nature (Under Review) 2024
Therapeutic Effects of Mesenchymal Stem Cell Homing in Craniofacial Bone Regeneration

I worked on this paper with Dr. Marwa Abbass and her team. Dr. Abbass is a professor of oral biology at the Faculty of Dentistry at Cairo University, where she also co-founded the Engineering and Stem Cell Unit. She holds the same professorship at the German University in Cairo. The paper is currently under review at Springer Nature.

My role was in the literature review. I went through around 50 to 60 papers and pulled the relevant data — stem cell origin, growth factors and scaffolds used, study models, and outcomes — into a structured data table organized by author and year. I then wrote up the findings from each source into coherent summaries, grouping them into separate sections by theme.

I also contributed to the manuscript itself and did a full editorial pass focused on reducing the similarity report. Got it under 20%.

The subject matter — how MSCs home to sites of craniofacial bone damage and support regeneration — sits right at the intersection of dentistry and regenerative medicine. It's an area that's moving fast and I happened to get a close look at it early.

Notebook

Winter 2025 Reflection
Staying late for emergency surgeries taught me more than I expected

There's a different energy in a dental office at 8pm when no one planned to be there. The patient is stressed, the doctor is focused in a way that doesn't leave room for small talk, and everyone just has to function. I've stayed for a few of those now and it's become one of the more useful parts of this job.

You learn a lot about how people operate under pressure by watching them do it. Dr. Furnari doesn't change much. That's actually the whole lesson — the goal is to be the kind of person who doesn't need perfect conditions to do good work.

Fall 2024 Research Note
What reading 50 papers on stem cells actually feels like

It's not glamorous. Most of it is the same information said differently, with slightly different study models and slightly different outcomes, and your job is to figure out what the differences actually mean versus what's just noise. That takes a while to get comfortable with.

What I found interesting was how much the scaffold matters — not just the stem cell itself, but what you put it in and how you get it to the site. A lot of the variation in outcomes across papers came down to that. MSC homing is the cell finding its way to where it's needed, and engineering that process is where the real work is happening right now.

I came into this knowing almost nothing about regenerative medicine. By the end I had opinions about it. That felt like something.

Summer 2024 Observation
Working with dementia patients changed how I think about patience

You can explain something clearly, demonstrate it correctly, and have someone look at you with complete understanding — and then thirty seconds later they don't remember any of it. That used to frustrate me more than it does now.

What I eventually figured out is that the point isn't whether they retain the information. The point is the interaction itself. Whether the person feels cared for in that moment. That's what they actually need, and it has nothing to do with whether the exercise gets remembered.

I think about that in dental contexts too. A patient being nervous doesn't mean they're being difficult. They're just scared. Reading that correctly matters.

Spring 2024 Thought
Why dentistry and not medicine

I get asked this a lot. The honest answer is that I find the specificity appealing. Dentistry has a defined scope, and within that scope there's more depth than most people realize. The oral-systemic connection — the research linking periodontal disease to cardiovascular disease, diabetes, lung cancer — is still being mapped out. It's not a finished field.

There's also the craft side of it. I've spent enough time in a dental office now to know that the manual precision required is real, and that it takes years to develop. I like that. I like that there's something to actually get better at, not just conceptually but with your hands.
